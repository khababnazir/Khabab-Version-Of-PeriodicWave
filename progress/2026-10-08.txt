1. AN EXACT ANSWER TO AIM AT (exact_noninteracting.py)

   Without Coulomb repulsion the electrons are independent, so the exact
   ground state is one Slater determinant of the 6 lowest one-electron
   states. Diagonalising h = -1/2 Laplacian + V_moire in plane waves on the
   9-site supercell gives
       E0 = -10.5277541 code units = -35.537 meV  (-5.923 meV per electron)
   converged to about 2e-9 code units (cutoff 30 versus 60). Lowest levels:
   -3.5519 (one state), then -1.39517 six times. Five electrons share the
   sixfold level, so the ground state is degenerate (an open shell) -- all
   six choices have the same energy. This connects to the sixfold Fermi
   liquid degeneracy in the paper's Appendix E.

2. THE GRADIENT, AND THE IMAGINARY-PART QUESTION (gradient.py,
   check_phase_gradient.py)

   dE/dtheta = 2 Re < (E_loc - E) conj(O) >,  O = d log|Psi| + i d phase.
   With a real E_loc the term 2 < Im E_loc * d phase/dtheta > is lost.
   Checked with no Monte Carlo at all: one electron, a 64x64 grid as exact
   quadrature, gradient compared with an independently differentiated
   energy. Relative error versus exact:
       full estimator                          1.6e-14
       real part of E_loc only                 0.687
       authors' loss.py, clipping on, complex  1.6e-14  (nothing clipped)
       authors' loss.py, clipping off, complex 0.687    (counts the term twice)
       any of them, real wave function         1.7e-14
   The clipping-off branch is inherited unchanged from DeepMind's FermiNet;
   the moire config does not reach it (it clips at 5). The clipping-on
   branch has the correct phase coefficient for complex E_loc, verified in
   the limit where clipping changes no values; real clipping biases the
   gradient on purpose, for lower variance.

3. THE NATURAL GRADIENT (gradient.py)

   Exact stochastic reconfiguration: build the quantum geometric tensor S
   (2624 x 2624 for our network), solve (S + damping) x = gradient. KFAC,
   which the authors use, approximates exactly this for large networks
   (the paper's ~90k parameters would give an S with ~8e9 entries); we use
   KFAC itself, through kfac-jax, from Stage 8. Two numerical points:
   negative eigenvalues of S are float32 rounding (the most negative is
   1.3e-7 times the largest, i.e. float32 precision) but exceed the damping,
   so they are set to zero before inverting; and the step limit is
   step.(S + damping).step <= max_step_norm, kfac-jax's norm constraint.

My answers to the two checkpoint questions (2026-10-08):

### 1. Why Dropping $\text{Im}(E_L)$ Leaves Reported Energy Correct, but Hurts the Optimizer

The reported energy is an **expectation value**, while the optimizer relies on **covariances**.

#### The Expectation Value

For any Hermitian Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \hat{H}^\dagger$, the exact mathematical expectation value is real:


$$\langle E \rangle = \int \vert{}\psi(R)\vert{}^2 E_L(R) \, dR \in \mathbb{R}$$


Because the true imaginary part integrates to zero, $\langle \text{Im}(E_L) \rangle = 0$. In Monte Carlo sampling, the imaginary part of the sample mean is just zero-mean stochastic noise. Discarding it when computing $\langle E \rangle$ leaves the estimated energy completely unbiased and correct.

#### The Variational Gradient

The energy gradient with respect to a real parameter $\theta_k$ is derived from:


$$\nabla_{\theta_k} \langle E \rangle = \nabla_{\theta_k} \frac{\langle \psi \vert{} \hat{H} \vert{} \psi \rangle}{\langle \psi \vert{} \psi \rangle}$$

Writing $\psi(R)$ in polar form $\psi(R) = e^{\ln \vert{}\psi(R)\vert{} + i \phi(R)}$, the logarithmic derivative is complex:


$$O_k(R) \equiv \frac{\partial \ln \psi(R)}{\partial \theta_k} = \frac{\partial \ln \vert{}\psi(R)\vert{}}{\partial \theta_k} + i \frac{\partial \phi(R)}{\partial \theta_k} = u_k(R) + i v_k(R)$$


where $u_k = \text{Re}(O_k)$ affects the **amplitude** (magnitude) and $v_k = \text{Im}(O_k)$ affects the **phase**.

Similarly, write the local energy in terms of its real and imaginary parts:


$$E_L(R) = E_R(R) + i E_I(R)$$

The exact energy gradient evaluated over the distribution $\vert{}\psi\vert{}^2$ is:


$$\nabla_{\theta_k} \langle E \rangle = 2 \, \text{Re} \, \mathbb{E}\left[ \big(E_L(R) - \langle E \rangle\big)^* O_k(R) \right]$$

Expanding the complex product inside the expectation:


$$\big(E_R - \langle E \rangle - i E_I\big) \big(u_k + i v_k\big) = \Big[(E_R - \langle E \rangle) u_k + E_I v_k\Big] + i \Big[(E_R - \langle E \rangle) v_k - E_I u_k\Big]$$

Taking the real part yields:


$$\nabla_{\theta_k} \langle E \rangle = 2 \, \mathbb{E}\Big[(E_R - \langle E \rangle) u_k\Big] + 2 \, \mathbb{E}\Big[E_I v_k\Big]$$

Look closely at the second term:


$$2 \, \mathbb{E}\Big[\text{Im}(E_L) \cdot \text{Im}(O_k)\Big] = 2 \, \text{cov}\Big(\text{Im}(E_L), \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta_k}\Big)$$

Even though $\mathbb{E}[\text{Im}(E_L)] = 0$, the correlation between $\text{Im}(E_L)$ and the phase gradients $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta_k}$ is **not zero**.

If you drop $\text{Im}(E_L)$ inside the optimizer:

1. You set this second term to zero.
2. The optimizer loses the gradient component that informs how parameters affect the **quantum phase** $\phi(R)$.
3. Because changes in phase directly alter the kinetic energy ($-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 \psi$), the optimizer follows a distorted, incorrect gradient direction. It stalls at an artificial plateau with higher variational energy.

---

### 2. Why the Real-Part-Only Gradient Is Exact When $\psi$ Is Real

When the wavefunction $\psi(R)$ is strictly real-valued:

1. **The Phase is Trivial:** $\phi(R) = 0$ (or constant $\pi$), which means:

$$v_k(R) = \text{Im}(O_k(R)) = \frac{\partial \phi(R)}{\partial \theta_k} \equiv 0$$



The log-derivatives $O_k(R)$ are entirely real.
2. **The Local Energy is Strictly Real:**

$$E_L(R) = \frac{\hat{H}\psi(R)}{\psi(R)} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\nabla^2 \psi(R)}{\psi(R)} + V(R)$$



If $\hat{H}$ is real (no magnetic field or complex gauge potential) and $\psi(R)$ is real, then $\nabla^2 \psi(R)$ and $V(R)$ are strictly real everywhere in configuration space. Therefore:

$$E_I(R) = \text{Im}(E_L(R)) \equiv 0 \quad \text{pointwise}$$



Now revisit the gradient expansion:


$$\nabla_{\theta_k} \langle E \rangle = 2 \, \mathbb{E}\Big[(E_R - \langle E \rangle) u_k\Big] + 2 \, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[E_I v_k\Big]}_{= 0}$$

Because both $E_I = 0$ and $v_k = 0$ hold identically at every point $R$, no non-zero term was discarded. The real-part formula is no longer an approximation—it is mathematically identical to the full expression.
